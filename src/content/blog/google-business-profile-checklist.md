Almost every contractor has a Google Business Profile. Almost none of them have optimized it.

There's a real difference between "claimed" and "optimized," and that gap is usually the exact reason a competitor with worse work shows up above you when someone searches for what you do. Google rewards profiles that look active, complete, and trustworthy. Most businesses set theirs up once, years ago, and never touch it again.

This post is the full checklist. Go through it item by item — most businesses are missing at least half of it.

## What is the "map pack," and why should you care?

When someone searches "roofer near me" or "plumber in [your city]," Google shows a map with three business listings above all the regular search results. That's called the **map pack** (sometimes the "3-pack").

Here's the number that should get your attention: most people searching on their phone never scroll past those three listings. If you're not in the map pack, it usually doesn't matter how great your website is — most searchers will never see it.

## Step 1: Fill in every category, not just one

Google lets you pick a primary category and several secondary ones. Most businesses only fill in the primary category and stop.

**Example:** A roofer who also does gutters and siding should be listed under all three categories — Roofing Contractor, Gutter Contractor, and Siding Contractor — not just "Roofing Contractor." Each category helps you show up for different searches.

**Do this today:** Open your profile, go to "Business Information," and add every category that honestly applies to your work. It takes two minutes.

## Step 2: Add real photos — and keep adding them

Stock photos and a five-year-old logo don't help you rank, and they don't build trust with someone scrolling on their phone at 9pm with a leaky ceiling.

What actually helps:

- **Before-and-after job photos.** Real proof beats any words you could write.
- **Photos of your crew and trucks.** People hire people, not logos.
- **New photos on a regular schedule**, not just one big upload the day you set up the profile.

**Do this today:** Upload at least 10 real job photos this week. Set a phone reminder to add 2-3 more every month going forward.

## Step 3: List your services individually

Don't lump everything into one vague paragraph. Google treats "roof repair," "roof replacement," and "storm damage inspection" as three separate searches. If your profile only mentions "roofing services" as one blob of text, you're invisible for all three of those specific searches.

**Do this today:** Go to the "Services" section and add each service you offer as its own line item, with a short, plain description of what it includes.

## Step 4: Post updates on a regular schedule

Google Posts is a feature almost no one uses — which means using it well is an easy way to stand out. A Google Post is like a mini social-media update that shows up right on your profile.

**What to post:**

- A recent job you finished (with a photo)
- A seasonal promotion or reminder ("Get your AC checked before summer")
- A quick tip related to your trade

**Do this today:** Post one update. Then put a recurring reminder on your calendar to post one every 2-4 weeks. A profile that posts regularly looks active. An active profile signals to Google — and to homeowners — that you're a real, running business.

## Step 5: Seed your own Q&A section

Anyone can ask a question on your Google Business Profile — and anyone can answer it. That includes strangers. That includes competitors.

**Do this today:** Think of the five questions customers ask you most often ("Do you offer free estimates?" "Are you licensed and insured?" "What areas do you serve?"). Post each one as a question yourself, then answer it yourself with a clear, honest answer. This stops a random stranger — or a competitor — from answering it first, badly.

## Step 6: Respond to every review — good and bad

A profile with 40 reviews and zero responses from the owner looks abandoned, even if the reviews themselves are great. Responding to reviews, especially the negative ones, is one of the strongest trust signals a small business can send.

**How to respond to a bad review (a simple template):**

> "Thanks for the feedback, [Name]. We're sorry this wasn't the experience we aim for. We'd like to make it right — please call us at [phone number] so we can sort this out directly."

Calm, short, and offers a real next step. Don't argue in public. Move the conversation to a phone call.

**How to respond to a good review (a simple template):**

> "Thank you, [Name]! We really appreciate you taking the time to write this, and we're glad we could help with [specific detail from their review]."

Mentioning a specific detail shows you actually read it — and it's not fake or copy-pasted, which people can tell.

## Why review freshness matters more than review count

Here's something most business owners don't realize: a business with 40 reviews and steady new ones coming in every month often outranks a business with 200 reviews and nothing new in over a year. Google reads "recent activity" as a signal that you're still a real, running business. This is exactly why review velocity — a steady stream of new reviews, not just a big pile of old ones — matters so much.

## The full checklist, all in one place

Print this or save it. Go through it once a month.

- [ ] Every relevant category added (not just the primary one)
- [ ] At least 10 real job photos uploaded, with new ones added monthly
- [ ] Every service listed as its own line item, not lumped together
- [ ] A Google Post published in the last 2-4 weeks
- [ ] Common questions seeded in the Q&A section, answered by you
- [ ] Every review responded to within a few days
- [ ] Business hours, phone number, and service area double-checked for accuracy
- [ ] Website link and booking link both working and up to date
