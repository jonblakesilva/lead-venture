Ask most contractors what it costs them to get a new customer and you'll get a shrug. That's not a knock — it's just not a number anyone taught them to track. But it's one of the few numbers that tells you, in plain terms, whether your marketing is actually working or just feels busy.

## The formula (it's simpler than it sounds)

**CAC = Total Marketing Spend ÷ Number of New Customers**

Spend $3,000 on ads and other marketing in a month, land 30 new customers, and your CAC is $100. That's it. The math isn't the hard part — knowing what to do with the number is.

## What's a "good" CAC?

There's no universal answer, but a useful rule of thumb: target a CAC around 10-20% of your average job value. If your average job is $1,800, you want to be acquiring customers for somewhere in the $180-$360 range. Above that, you're either overspending on the wrong channels or your close rate is too low to make the spend efficient.

This isn't a hard ceiling — a $360 CAC to land a customer worth $15,000 over a few years is obviously fine. Which is exactly why CAC only means something next to a second number.

## The number that makes CAC useful: LTGP

Lifetime Gross Profit (LTGP) is the total profit a customer generates over the whole relationship, not just the first job. A one-off residential customer might be worth 2-3 jobs over time. A recurring commercial account can be worth 5-10x that. Once you know LTGP, the real rule shows up:

**You can afford to spend 30-50% of LTGP to acquire a customer.**

That's why a roofer chasing a property-management account can justify a much higher CAC than the same roofer chasing a single homeowner with one leaking flashing — the accounts aren't the same customer, even though the acquisition channel might look identical on a spreadsheet.

## Where contractors actually get this wrong

- **Tracking spend but not conversions.** Knowing you spent $3,000 on Google Ads means nothing without knowing how many of those clicks became paying jobs.
- **Averaging across channels that aren't comparable.** A $50 CAC from referrals and a $300 CAC from a lead-aggregator site are not the same kind of customer, and blending them into one number hides which channel is actually working.
- **Never calculating LTGP at all**, which means every acquisition decision gets made off the first invoice instead of the real value of the relationship.

## The fix is a five-minute exercise, not a new tool

Pull last month's total marketing spend. Count new customers from that spend. Divide. Then do the same rough math for LTGP on a typical job. You'll usually find one of two things: either you're spending less than you could afford to on the channels that are actually working, or you're spending real money on a channel that's barely breaking even. Both are useful to know, and neither requires new software to find out.
